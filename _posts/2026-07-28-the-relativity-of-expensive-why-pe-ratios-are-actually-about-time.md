If you spend enough time reading financial news, you will inevitably stumble into a debate about whether a stock or the entire market is "too expensive".

Usually, this argument centers around the Price-to-Earnings (P/E) ratio. A traditional value investor might look at an S&P 500 P/E of 35x and declare a bubble. A tech growth investor might look at that same number and see a perfectly reasonable entry point.

Who is right? According to Morgan Housel in *The Psychology of Money*, they both might be, because investors are often playing completely different games.

The key to understanding market valuations isn't just looking at the price. It is understanding that a P/E ratio is fundamentally a measure of time.

### The Earnings Payback Period

At its core, a P/E of 35 means that if a company's earnings never change, it will take exactly 35 years for the company to earn back the price you paid for its stock.

But earnings are rarely static. As earnings grow, that 35-year horizon compresses. The exact mathematical intersection where cumulative growing earnings equal your initial purchase price is called the Earnings Payback Period.

When you introduce growth into the equation, the debate over what constitutes an "expensive" stock completely changes shape. Let's look at what annual growth rate different investors need to justify buying into a P/E of 35:

**The Strict Value Investor (3-Year Horizon):** To earn back a 35x multiple in just three years, the market would need to grow earnings by a staggering ~190% per year. To this investor, a P/E of 35 is undeniably a massive bubble.

**The Growth Investor (10-Year Horizon):** To earn back the price in a decade, the required growth rate drops to ~22% per year. That's tough for a broad market index, but highly achievable for an elite software or semiconductor company.

**The Long-Term Indexer (15-Year Horizon):** If you are willing to wait 15 years, you only need earnings to grow by ~10% per year—which happens to align perfectly with historical average market returns. To this investor, a P/E of 35 is simply business as usual.

They are all looking at the exact same asset, at the exact same price. The only variable changing is their patience.

### When "Cheap" Also Depends on Time

The same math runs in reverse for low multiples. At the time of writing, Volkswagen trades around a trailing P/E of ~5. Flat earnings would pay back the purchase price in five years—so the stock looks "cheap" by the usual shorthand. But cheap to *whom*?

**The Strict Value Investor (3-Year Horizon):** To earn back 5x in three years, earnings still need to grow by ~28% per year. A short horizon does not make a low multiple free; it still prices in growth.

**The Long-Term Indexer (15-Year Horizon):** Stretch the payback to fifteen years and the required growth flips negative—about −16% per year. In other words, a patient buyer only "breaks even" on earnings payback if the business shrinks for a decade and a half. At a 10-year horizon the implied path is still roughly −13% annual earnings decline.

So a P/E of 5 is not automatically a bargain, just as a P/E of 35 is not automatically a bubble. The short-horizon investor is still underwriting growth; the long-horizon investor is underwriting the market's pessimism that earnings will erode. ### The Delusion of the Dot-Com Bubble

This mathematical framework also helps us separate rational optimism from historical delusions.

During the dot-com bubble of 1999 and 2000, it wasn't uncommon for tech darlings to trade at trailing P/E ratios of 100 or more. If a long-term growth investor bought a stock at a 100 P/E and gave it a generous 10-year horizon, they were mathematically betting that the company would grow its net earnings by 47% every single year, for a decade straight.

In the history of capitalism, almost no mega-cap company has ever sustained that level of growth for that long. The math was broken. The market wasn't pricing in optimism; it was pricing in impossibilities.

Today, when we see companies trading at high multiples, the Earnings Payback math allows us to ask a much better question. Instead of asking, "Is this expensive?" we should ask, "What growth trajectory is required for my specific time horizon, and is that trajectory actually possible?"
